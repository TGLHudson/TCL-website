What it argues

The ladder, read as fit

The safety culture ladder has been in use for more than two decades. It underpins the Energy Institute's Hearts and Minds programme, it was adapted for the United Kingdom's National Health Service, and in the Netherlands it became a national certification scheme. What it has lacked is an account of why it works: why the rungs sit where they do, and why the steps between them are hard in the particular ways they are hard.

The book gives that account. Each rung is a different way of deciding who acts on risk, with its own structures: the law's rules at Reactive, authority held in the line at Calculative, written triggers that hand authority to the people at the work at Proactive, shared premises at Generative. Pathological sits beneath them all, the place every rung's dark side leads if nothing stops it.

From that account comes the book's central claim. A safe organisation is the one whose culture matches the risk it is already running, held with a margin above the floor. Height for its own sake is cost and ceremony; a culture below the floor is where incidents accumulate.

The five rungs

From not wanting to know to chronic unease

  • GenerativeAuthority moves with events, under premises everyone shares.
  • ProactiveTriggers set in advance hand decisions to the people who can see the conditions.
  • CalculativeA real management system, run with discipline; at risk of mistaking the dashboard for the world.
  • ReactiveSerious and energetic, mostly after the event.
  • PathologicalBad news has nowhere to go, so the organisation believes it is safe.

The ladder originates with Dr P. T. W. Hudson, the author's father, building on Ron Westrum's three cultures.

Who it is for

Anyone running a risk space

The evidence comes from industrial safety: drilling, aviation maintenance, construction, rail and healthcare. The argument reaches further. If you run a hospital ward, a bank's risk function, a software platform or a logistics operation, you are running a risk space, and the four questions apply.

One chapter is addressed to the parties around the operation: the board, the workforce and its representatives, and the regulator.
